Deep Research: The Psychology of Cartoon-in-Reality Advertising
Executive Summary
Your "House Theory" is largely well-supported by cognitive psychology and advertising science, with several important nuances and one significant caution. The core insight—keep the world real and the intruders impossible—maps cleanly onto the established distinction between the von Restorff effect (perceptual isolation) and the bizarreness effect (semantic incongruity). The research strongly validates your mixed-media approach, your 10-second compression strategy, and your unresolved-narrative tactic. The Zeigarnik-based "cognitive itch" claim needs tempering: the attentional pull of unfinished business is robust, but the memory advantage is disputed by a 2025 meta-analysis. The PSYOP footnote remains speculative but not unfounded. Below is the full audit, with citations, caveats, and tactical upgrades for your campaign.
1. The Bizarreness Effect — Validated, With Critical Conditions
What the research confirms
The bizarreness effect is real and well-documented. McDaniel and Einstein (1986) formalized it as a specific case of distinctiveness in memory, building on Delin's first empirical study in 1968.  The mechanism is elaborative processing: when the brain encounters conceptual incongruity, it works harder to resolve it, creating stronger memory traces. 
Recent research extends this into the visual domain. Morita & Kambara (2022) found that objects in bizarre/expectation-incongruent colors (e.g., a blue banana) are recalled better than ordinary ones, supporting the "distinctiveness account"—expectation-incongruent information receives prioritized elaboration at encoding. 
The mixed-list condition — your most important constraint
This is where your "House Theory" earns its keep. The bizarreness effect only works when bizarre and ordinary material appear side by side. In pure-list designs (where everything is bizarre), the advantage disappears entirely.  McDaniel and Einstein (1986) established this, and it has replicated consistently for three decades.
Translation to your campaign: A photoreal office with a hand-drawn cartoon dog platoon is a mixed-list frame. The real world is the ordinary list; the cartoon is the bizarre item. If you made the entire ad cartoon, or if every competitor ad in the break also used cartoon intruders, your advantage would collapse. Your "Roger Rabbit rules" are not stylistic preference—they are a mandatory condition for the effect to operate.
Free recall vs. recognition — know your metric
Bizarreness improves free recall (unaided, top-of-mind) significantly, cued recall weakly, and recognition memory often not at all.  This means your style-clash strategy is optimized for: "Have you heard of [Brand]?" not for: "Which of these brands did you see an ad for?" If your KPI is aided awareness, bizarreness is the wrong lever. If your KPI is unaided recall and word-of-mouth, it is the right one.
Mental imagery multiplier
Bizarre items that evoke vivid mental images produce stronger recall. Toyota (2002) found high-imagery bizarre sentences were recalled significantly better than low-imagery ones.  Visual bizarreness (an animal performing a human activity, an impossible physical transformation) outperforms conceptual bizarreness. Your cartoon dogs dropping onto drafting nerds is high-imagery visual bizarreness—the strongest form.
2. The Von Restorff Effect (1933) — Your Frame-by-Frame Hook
Distinction from bizarreness
The von Restorff effect (isolation effect) operates through any form of contrast—color, size, position. The bizarreness effect requires conceptual/semantic violation.  Your campaign uses both simultaneously: the cartoon style is a perceptual isolate (von Restorff) within the photoreal frame, and the cartoon dogs' behavior is a semantic violation (bizarreness).
Every frame carries the hook
Your claim that "every frame carries the hook" is neurologically defensible. The von Restorff effect predicts that the one item that doesn't match the list is the one everybody remembers.  In your ads, the cartoon element is the isolated item in every shot. Because the isolation is stylistic (not just a single prop or color), it persists across cuts, giving you a von Restorff advantage in every frame rather than just one "hero moment."
3. The Cognitive Itch & Zeigarnik Effect — Attention Yes, Memory Maybe
The established mechanism
Bluma Zeigarnik (1927) found that interrupted tasks were remembered roughly twice as well as completed ones.  The mechanism is cognitive tension: an unfinished task creates a "quasi-need" that stays mentally active until closure. 
The 2025 meta-analysis caveat
This is the most important update to your theory. A 2025 meta-analysis by Ghibellini and Meier found no reliable recall advantage for interrupted tasks once Zeigarnik's own 1927 data was excluded.  What does survive is: (1) the pull to resume an interrupted task, and (2) the way unfinished goals intrude on attention. 
Translation: Your "cognitive itch" is better framed as an attentional intrusion mechanism, not a memory enhancement mechanism. The viewer's brain doesn't necessarily remember the ad better because one thread is unresolved; rather, the unresolved thread keeps the ad active in working memory, increasing the chance of re-engagement (rewatching, talking about it, searching for it). The "replay engine" you describe is real, but it runs on attention persistence, not recall amplification.
The "leave one thread unresolved" rule
This remains excellent creative guidance. The principle behind the Zeigarnik effect—our brain's automatic tendency to fill gaps and create coherent wholes from incomplete patterns—drives narrative engagement.  In advertising, incomplete information creates curiosity that motivates people to seek closure.  Your rule—"the ad should almost make sense"—is a precise application of partial incongruity resolution.
The neuroscience of resolution
fMRI research on humor processing provides a direct neural map for your strategy. Complete incongruity resolution (where everything makes sense) activates the temporo-parietal junction (integration), precuneus (perspective-taking), posterior cingulate cortex (episodic memory), and parahippocampal gyrus (funny feeling). 
Partial/absurd resolution (where one thread stays unresolved) activates different circuits: the fusiform gyrus (word processing), inferior frontal gyrus, and superior temporal gyrus (pragmatic awareness).  The brain knows the absurdity is unresolvable and processes it with "pragmatic awareness"—essentially, it tags the content as intentionally strange rather than confusing. This is exactly your sweet spot: the viewer recognizes the cartoon dogs as deliberate absurdity, not a production error, and the unresolved thread (Why a tank? Why drafting nerds?) keeps the loop open.
4. Compression & Short-Form Advertising — Strongly Supported
The template theory of memory
MediaScience and the University of South Australia coined the "template theory of memory": most of the effect of advertisement length on awareness is delivered in the first five seconds of exposure.  In their study of 722 participants testing 7-, 15-, 30-, and 60-second variants, 7-second ads were as effective as 15-second ads for ad recall. 
Diminishing returns
Goldstein, McAfee & Suri (2011) found that memory response follows a logarithmic curve of exposure time: sharp increases in the first 10 seconds, then diminishing returns.  This validates your "10 seconds is the perfect dose" claim. You get the majority of the memory impact in the first 5–10 seconds; everything beyond that has sharply declining marginal value.
Frequency and repetition
Your "six times in one break for the price of one long ad" strategy is supported by frequency research. 15-second ads deliver approximately 75–80% of the memory-based effectiveness of 30-second spots.  6-second bumper ads capture 8–11% more attention per second than longer formats and deliver ~60% of a 30-second ad's impact. 
Shorter ads also drive more site visits: QuickFrame by MNTN found 15-second ads delivered 46% better performance than 30-second ads for visit rate. 
Tactical upgrade: Your 10-second spots are in the optimal zone. Consider running them as reminders of a longer "anchor" ad (e.g., a 30-second version for first exposure, 10-second versions for frequency). MediaScience found that 7-second ads are as effective as 15-second ads as reminders of longer advertisements. 
The 80s/90s toy commercial parallel
While direct academic research on 80s/90s toy commercial psychology is sparse, the historical context supports your claim. The deregulation of children's advertising in the 1980s created a flood of short-form toy ads embedded in cartoon programming.  These ads were engineered for repetition and "mini-movie" rewatchability—fast cuts, clear product demonstration, and memorable jingles. The structural similarity to your 10-second spots is real: high compression, high repetition, and viewer-driven narrative completion.
5. The Psy-War Footnote — Speculative but Mechanistically Plausible
What we can verify
U.S. Army PSYOP units have produced billions of leaflets with imagery ranging from "chintzy fantasy" to "cartoonish" aesthetics.  Herbert A. Friedman, a retired PSYOP officer, noted that with modern CGI, "you lose a lot when you go CGI" and that speed often trumps artistic quality. 
The deliberate-memory hypothesis
There is no verified documentation that PSYOP designers used surreal imagery as a deliberate memory technique. The odd look of many leaflets appears to be a byproduct of rushed production, cultural translation errors, and software limitations rather than cognitive science. However, the mechanism you identify—incongruity gets encoded, plain doesn't—is solid. Whether the odd look was intentional or accidental, it would still ride on the same bizarreness/von Restorff mechanisms.
Verdict: Keep the footnote marked as speculation. The mechanism is real; the historical intent is unverified.
6. The "SOPSROES Rule" — Unverified in Literature
I could find no academic or industry reference to "SOPSROES" as an advertising principle. This appears to be an internal acronym or coinage. The underlying principle—"strange delivery, true message"—is, however, well-supported by credibility research. Truthful claims delivered in unexpected formats create a credibility contrast effect: the viewer's truth-detection systems are activated by the factual claim, while the strange delivery prevents habituation. Additional tactical recommendations from the research
Test for the vampire effect. Bizarreness can improve ad recall without improving brand recall.  Run unaided recall tests: when someone retells the ad, do they mention your brand name or just "that ad with the cartoon dogs"? If it's the latter, the bizarre element is structurally detached—tie it closer to the product (e.g., the dogs are using the bone button, not just appearing near it).
Humor + bizarreness = dual encoding. Macklin (2008) found that bizarreness combined with humor produces stronger delayed recall than either alone.  Your spots should be funny, not just strange.
Bizarreness is strongest at top-of-funnel. Deploy it for awareness and unaided recall. For conversion, transition to conventional credibility signals. 
The first 5 seconds are the ad. The template theory of memory says most awareness impact is delivered in the first 5 seconds.  Front-load your impossible image and your brand name.
Avoid negative/absurd valence. Your campaign uses positive absurdity (cartoon dogs, houses popping like toast). This is correct. Disturbing bizarreness improves recall but damages sentiment. 
8. Synthesis: The Unified Model
Your "House Theory" can be restated as a three-layer cognitive capture system:
Table
Layer	Mechanism	Your Execution
Perceptual capture	Von Restorff isolation effect	Cartoon style against photoreal world
Semantic capture	Bizarreness effect (elaborative processing)	Dogs dropping onto drafting nerds, tank following
Attention persistence	Zeigarnik open-loop tension	One unresolved thread per spot
Each layer operates on a different timescale: von Restorff works in milliseconds (first fixation), bizarreness works in seconds (encoding), Zeigarnik works in minutes to hours (intrusive thoughts). Your 10-second format is compressed enough to deliver all three layers before the skip button appears.
