# DRIVE-THRU WINDOW OPERATOR GUIDEBOOK

**Rough Drafter — front of house**
Rev 1, 31 Aug 2026. Written from the working engine, `gruff-interview.js`.

---

## 0. What this book is

This is the manual for the dog on the window. It covers **taking the order**:
how the client is asked, in what order, what an answer opens, what a silence
means, and what gets handed to the back.

It does **not** cover building the house. That's the *Home Plan Building
Guidebook*, and the two books never reach across. The window takes the order;
the kitchen builds it.

**Gruff is the first operator, not the only one.** He's perfecting the flow
before it goes to the rest of the shift. So every rule in here is written for
*whoever is on the window* — the script is the job, the voice is the dog.

> Professor Gruff, who writes notes in the model, is a different character
> doing a different job. Not this book. (Names to be sorted later; keeping
> them apart here so the rename is a rename.)

## 1. The shift, in one line

**The client can press the bone at any time and drive away with a whole house.**

Everything else in this book follows from that. Press the bone before Gruff has
said a word and a complete, sensible house comes out. Every question after
that only *tightens* what the bone would have built anyway.

That is the operator's core discipline: **you are never blocking the order.**
The client is not filling out a form to earn a house. They already have the
house. They're telling you how they'd like it.

### The four rules of the window

1. **The order is always complete.** At every rung, whatever nobody said falls
   to a crowd-pleasing default. There is no half-house.
2. **Never ask what you can already see.** The drawing answers plenty — storey
   count, whether stairs are placed, a door already sited. Asking is rude.
3. **No answer is a dead end.** "About three", "a couple", "3 bedrooms" and "3"
   all land. Anything genuinely unparseable comes back once, good-naturedly,
   never as an error.
4. **Remind, don't nag.** The bone is mentioned every fourth question — never
   on the first, never twice running, never instead of the question.

## 2. The order of service

### 2.1 The critical ladder — always first

Four questions, always in this order, because they move the most house:

| # | Ask | Why it's first |
|---|-----|----------------|
| 1 | How many floors? | Decides whether there's an upstairs at all — and therefore whether half the later questions exist |
| 2 | How many bedrooms? | Spawns a placement question per bedroom |
| 3 | And bathrooms? | Same — spawns per-bath questions |
| 4 | Which side is the front door? | Sets the compass everything else is described against |

Nothing else is asked until the ladder is done or settled.

### 2.2 Then the rooms the counts opened up

The tree goes **deep, not wide**. Say four bedrooms and you get four "which
side?" questions — a bigger house is a longer conversation, not a bigger
catalogue. This is deliberate: it keeps the question list small enough to hold
in your head while scaling to any house.

### 2.3 Then the detail

Primary suite and what hangs off it, kitchen, living, dining, laundry, drop
zone, pantry, office, closets, storage, windows. Order matters less here; the
client can bone out at any point and lose nothing important.

## 3. Reading the customer

### 3.1 The four kinds of answer

- **Count** — a number. Digits, words, or words inside a sentence.
- **Yes/no** — "no" is checked before "yes", so "not really" reads as no
  rather than falling through.
- **Zone** — one of five words, and only five: **front, back, left, right, by
  the stairs.** That's the entire spatial vocabulary. The client never points
  at the drawing and never gives a dimension.
- **Choice** — from a named list, matched loosely.

### 3.2 The five zones are the whole map

`front` is the street side, `back` the yard, `left` and `right` as you face
the house — and these are the *same* front and back as the elevation marks, so
a client saying "front" and a drafter reading E1 mean one thing.

This is the single most important constraint in the book. The client cannot say
"eleven feet from the west wall", and shouldn't be able to. Five words is
enough to describe any house to a builder, and it's few enough that nobody
gets it wrong. **Do not add a sixth zone without a very good reason.**

### 3.3 What the drawing already answers

Two levels of "already known", and they behave differently:

- **Settled and silent** — storeys, a placed stair, a placed front door. Not
  asked, not confirmed, not mentioned.
- **Settled and worth confirming** — bedroom and bathroom counts from project
  info. Asked as *"Project info has you down for 3 bedrooms. Still 3?"* rather
  than cold.

The difference is whether the drawing *knows* or merely *implies*. Geometry
knows. Project info implies.

## 4. Silence is a valid answer

Every unanswered question takes its default, and every default taken is
**named** in the order so the back of house can say what was decided for you.

That last part is the honesty clause and it is not optional. A client who
pressed the bone early is owed a list of what Gruff chose on their behalf.
A default that isn't disclosed is a decision taken behind someone's back.

### The standing defaults

Two storeys, three bedrooms, two baths, front entry. Primary suite at the back
with a three-piece ensuite and a walk-in. Kitchen back, living front, separate
dining room front. Pantry yes, drop zone yes, office no, storage no. Standard
closets, balanced windows.

That's a house most people would be happy with, which is the whole point of the
bone.

## 5. What gets handed to the back of house

The order ticket. Not a house — a **program**:

- **stamps** — every room, with its storey and its resolved position
- **storeys**
- **entry** side
- **windows** disposition
- **defaulted** — every question nobody answered
- **complete** — always true

The window resolves zone *words* into coordinates before handing over, so
there's exactly one place in the whole system where "back" becomes a number.
Companion rooms — ensuite, walk-in, closets — ride with their host room rather
than being placed independently.

**The window does not check whether the order can be built.** That's the rule
book's job, and it's what makes the two jobs separable.

## 6. Where the window and the kitchen disagree

The client asks for something the house rules won't allow. This will happen and
it is not a fault.

**The window takes the order anyway.** Record what was asked for, in the
client's words. The building guidebook is what says no, and *why*, and the
operator carries the message back.

Never argue at the window. The customer is describing a want; wants aren't
wrong. The plan is where the constraint lives.

### Known collision — open

The current defaults put **storage**, the **main bath**, the laundry and the
drop zone "by the stairs." The Home Plan Building Guidebook forbids storage and
bathrooms opening onto a stair.

So Gruff is presently ordering something the kitchen must refuse. The seam is
working — but the defaults should change so the order is buildable as taken:

- **Storage** — default it away from the stair.
- **Main bath** — default it away from the stair.
- **Laundry** — ruling needed. Utility room, walked into, not private. Movie's
  call.
- **Drop zone** — by the stairs is right and stays.

## 7. Voice

Short lines. A dot-matrix strip is not a paragraph.

He's the guide, so the register is a good counter hand who knows the menu — not
a salesman, not a form. The re-ask when something doesn't parse is
good-natured, never a correction: *"Say again for the old goat?"*

The opener varies but is **seeded**, so the same interview reads the same way
twice. Flavour that can't be reproduced can't be tested.

Voice is the one part of this book that belongs to the dog rather than the job.
When the next operator takes the window, this section is what they rewrite.
Everything above it, they inherit.
