# WHY THE MASONS KEPT SECRETS
### BONEYARD · HISTORY EDUCATION — research notes, dated and sourced
*(operative-era focus: the working stonemasons, before the gentleman's club era)*

---

## The short answer

The medieval stonemasons guarded two different kinds of secrets, for two very practical reasons:

1. **Trade protection.** Their core professional knowledge — how to derive a full 3D building from a 2D ground plan using geometry — was their livelihood. No patents, no copyright, no licensing boards existed. The *only* way to protect the craft's value (and its wage level) was to keep the method inside the lodge.
2. **Credential verification.** Masons were the rare medieval trade that *traveled*. A carpenter or baker lived and died in one town where everyone knew him; a cathedral mason walked from site to site across Europe. When a stranger showed up at your lodge claiming to be a trained fellow of the craft, you needed a way to check — with no papers, no guild registry you could consult, no way to write home. The secret words, grips, and salutations were a portable, unforgeable ID card.

Everything else — the oaths, the rituals, the closed lodge door — existed to protect those two things. The "society protection" layer (secrecy as defense against hostile authorities) came later and is real too, but it's the second chapter, not the first.

---

## Chapter 1 — The secret was GEOMETRY (the trade-protection story)

**The single best-documented secret in the whole history:** at Regensburg in **1459**, representatives of nearly all the German-speaking masons' lodges (under the four great head-lodges of **Strasbourg, Cologne, Vienna, and Bern**) met "in the manner of a chapter" and wrote down unified ordinances. Article 13 says, in plain words:

> "Also no workman, nor master, nor parlier, nor journeyman shall teach anyone, whatever he may be called, not being one of our handicraft and never having done mason work, **how to take the elevation from the ground plan.**"

That's it. The great secret, named in the lodge's own statute book: the geometric procedure for developing a building's vertical design (elevation) from its horizontal plan. In modern terms: **the secret was the drafting method.** Everything a drafter does when they project a section or elevation from a plan — that operation was, in 1459, a legally guarded trade secret punishable by expulsion from the craft.

- These 1459 statutes were confirmed by **Emperor Maximilian I in 1498**, giving the craft's secrecy imperial legal backing.
- The master of each lodge was required to keep the lodge book and **read it aloud to the masons once a year** — a secrecy regime with an annual refresher course.
- Sources: Paul Frankl, "The Secret of the Mediaeval Masons," *The Art Bulletin* vol. 27 (1945), https://doi.org/10.2307/3046979 ; the Strasbourg/Regensburg 1459 constitutions (English translation at themasonictrowel.com, "The Constitutions of the Masons of Strasburg 1459").

**Why geometry specifically?** Medieval master masons didn't do structural math — no statics, no load calculations. What they had instead were **proportional geometric recipes** (rotate the square, apply ad quadratum / ad triangulum constructions) that turned a plan into safe wall heights, vault curvatures, pinnacle tapers. Those recipes were the accumulated R&D of centuries of trial, error, and collapsed vaults. Give them away and anyone could underbid you; keep them and every prince who wanted a cathedral had to hire the brotherhood.

**Fun detail:** the masons were effectively medieval Europe's only *international* trade. The same Italian masons worked at Speyer in Germany and Lund in Sweden; French masons worked in Germany. That mobility is exactly why the credential problem (Chapter 2) mattered so much. (Frankl 1945.)

## Chapter 2 — The Mason Word (the ID-card story)

Because masons traveled, the craft developed **modes of recognition**: secret salutations, handshakes ("grips"), and words that proved a stranger's grade without any paperwork.

- The 1459 German ordinances already required the newly promoted journeyman to swear **not to reveal the secret salutation** used between craft members (Frankl 1945).
- In Scotland this became the famous **"Mason Word."** By **1707** the Mother Kilwinning Lodge (Ayrshire) was formally defining an outsider as a mason *"without the word"* (OED, entry "cowan," citing lodge records).
- **William Schaw**, Master of Works to King James VI, issued the **Schaw Statutes on 28 December 1598** (recorded in the minute book of the Lodge of Edinburgh) — the first national regulation of lodges as standing institutions, with wardens, apprentice limits, and competence rules ("no master shall take on work he cannot complete"). The 1598 statutes also order that **no master or fellow of craft receive any "cowanis" to work in his society**, under a £20 penalty. (Texts at skirret.com and ecossais.info; D. Murray Lyon, *History of the Lodge of Edinburgh*, 1873.)

**The COWAN** is the fun one: a Scots word for a dry-stone diker — a man who builds walls *without mortar* and, pointedly, without having served an apprenticeship. In lodge language it came to mean any uninitiated outsider, and by ~1742 English lodge documents describe an officer guarding the lodge door *"with a drawn Sword, from all Cowens and Eves-droppers."* The Tyler (the sword-holding doorkeeper) is still a Masonic office today — a fossil of the era when keeping the trade secrets literally meant guarding the door. (Dictionaries of the Scots Language, "cowan"; OED 1893 entry.)

**Mason's marks:** each mason carved a small personal geometric mark into his finished stones — part signature, part piece-work accounting, part quality control. You can still find them on medieval cathedrals; they're the era's equivalent of a commit history.

## Chapter 3 — Society protection (the keep-your-head-down story)

Secrecy also shielded the organization itself:

- **1425 (3 Henry VI, cap. 1):** the English Parliament outlawed the masons' "yearly congregations and confederacies," declaring that organizers "shall be judged for felons." The masons' annual assemblies were, functionally, wage-coordination meetings that undercut the Statutes of Labourers (medieval wage-cap laws passed after the Black Death made labor scarce and expensive). The craft largely ignored the ban — but it's a clear, dated case where *meeting at all* was criminal, and discretion was survival. (Statute text in Pickering's *Statutes at Large*; transcription at freemasonry.bcy.ca; ordinance also recorded in the London Letter-Books, August 1425, british-history.ac.uk.)
- The masons' oaths were sworn on religious relics, their assemblies were called "chapters" (a term borrowed from Benedictine monastery meetings — no other trade guild used it), and their wealth, mobility, and connections made outsiders suspicious. Mystery was partly self-defense against that suspicion, and partly — let's be honest — good branding. (Strasbourg 1459 commentary; Talk.Build, "Why stonemasons were once considered a secret society.")
- Later, after the craft went speculative, secrecy took on genuinely political stakes (papal condemnation from 1738 onward, bans under various regimes) — but that's past the operative era.

## Chapter 4 — The paper trail (documents worth knowing, in order)

| Date | Document | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| c. **1390** | **Regius Poem / Halliwell MS** (British Library, Royal MS 17 A I) | Oldest masonic document: 64 vellum pages of Middle English rhyming couplets. Legendary history (Euclid "counterfeited geometry and called it masonry"; craft brought to England under King Athelstan, r. 924–939), then 15 Articles for masters + 15 Points for craftsmen — a complete medieval code of site conduct: teach apprentices properly, don't take jobs you can't do, don't make masons work at night, don't harbor thieves. Dated ~1390 by British Museum experts; publicized by James O. Halliwell in 1840; the "Regius" name comes from its stay in the Royal Library until George II gave it to the British Museum in 1757. |
| **1425** | Statute 3 Henry VI c.1 | Masons' annual assemblies banned as felony (see Ch. 3). |
| **1459** | Regensburg/Strasbourg Ordinances | The geometry secret named in writing (see Ch. 1). Confirmed by Emperor Maximilian I, 1498. |
| **28 Dec 1598** | First Schaw Statutes (Edinburgh) | Lodges become permanent regulated institutions; cowans banned (see Ch. 2). |
| **1646** | Elias Ashmole's diary | First recorded initiation of an English *speculative* (non-working) mason, at Warrington — the craft is already admitting gentlemen antiquarians. (UGLE, "History of Freemasonry.") |
| **24 June 1717** | Goose and Gridiron alehouse, St Paul's Churchyard, London | Four London lodges declare themselves a Grand Lodge and elect Anthony Sayer Grand Master — the traditional birthday of organized modern Freemasonry. Worth knowing: the only source is James Anderson's *Book of Constitutions* (1738), written 21 years later, and modern scholars (Prescott & Sommers) argue the Grand Lodge really consolidated around the Duke of Montagu's election in **1721**. Even the founding date of the era of *open* masonry is itself a bit of a mystery. (UGLE; Prescott, "Searching for the apple tree," Univ. of Glasgow eprints.) |

## Chapter 5 — Fun stories for the BONEYARD plaques

**The Apprentice Pillar (Rosslyn Chapel, Scotland, chapel begun 1446).** The master mason, before carving the chapel's final and finest pillar, traveled abroad to study the great models. While he was gone his apprentice dreamed the design and carved it himself — a helix of vines with eight dragons gnawing cords at the base. The master returned, saw a pillar better than anything he could make, and killed the boy with a single mallet blow to the forehead. The other masons carved both heads into the chapel walls — the apprentice with his gash, and the master placed so his stone gaze rests on the apprentice's pillar forever. The tale is documented from at least **12 August 1677** (Thomas Kirk's travel diary). It's the craft's own cautionary myth about jealousy — and about what a talented apprentice with one good dream can do. (rosslynchapel.com, "The Apprentice Pillar" and "Early Visitors.")

**The Four Crowned Martyrs (Quattuor Coronati).** The patron saints of the stonemasons: four Roman craftsmen-martyrs (trad. c. 302 AD, under Diocletian) executed — in the masonic telling — for refusing to carve a pagan idol. The Regius Poem (~1390) includes their legend, and the German lodges invoked "the holy four crowned martyrs of everlasting memory" in the opening line of the 1459 Strasbourg constitutions. The premier masonic research lodge in the world, founded London 1886, is still named Quatuor Coronati Lodge No. 2076. Craftsmen who died rather than misuse the craft — that's the oldest story the lodges told about themselves.

**"Read the book aloud once a year."** The 1459 ordinances made the lodge master read the rule-book to the assembled masons annually. Secrets rot if nobody re-learns them; the lodge institutionalized the refresher. (Frankl 1945.)

**The drawn sword at the door.** From at least the early 1700s, lodges posted a Tyler with a drawn sword to keep off "all Cowans and Eves-droppers" — the most literal possible image of guarding trade knowledge. (OED "cowan" citations, c. 1742.)
